# Research brief: naval forces, undersea, electronic, space, cyber and information warfare

Prepared 19 Nov 2026 to audit naval, surveillance, EW, cyber and intelligence mechanics. Open-source, capability/role-level only: no tactics, targeting, vulnerabilities or techniques. Every bullet carries one label: [Fact] officially documented or widely confirmed; [Assessment] expert or official estimate/analysis; [Contested] sources disagree or evidence is thin (the disagreement is stated); [Speculative] projection or scenario assumption. Bracketed numbers point to the numbered Sources list. Access caveat: the 2025 DoD China report PDF, USNI News and some Diplomat pages were blocked to my fetch tool, so DoD figures are cited through CRS and Erickson; sources marked "(search only)" were seen in search results but not opened.

## C. Naval forces

### C1. Balance of forces: sea denial, not just ship-versus-ship
- [Assessment] DoD credits the PLA Navy (PLAN) with "over 370" ships and submarines, including more than 140 major surface combatants and excluding about 60 Houbei missile boats; DoD projected 395 ships by 2025 and 435 by 2030 (CRS RL33153, 2025 [1]; Naval News, 2023 [4]).
- [Fact] The US Navy had 296 battle-force ships on 30 Sep 2024 (CRS RL33153, 2025 [1]).
- [Assessment] A US Navy slide put PRC shipbuilding capacity at about 230 times US capacity by gross tonnage; CRS cautions that this mostly reflects simple commercial ships and the ratio for complex warships "may be far less" (CRS RL33153, 2025 [1]).
- [Assessment] CRS/DoD describe the PLAN as an anti-access/area-denial force meant to deter or delay US intervention, backed by land-based anti-ship ballistic missiles (DF-21D over 1,500 km; DF-26 3,000-4,000 km) and the YJ-21 (CRS RL33153, 2025 [1]).
- [Assessment] DoD judges the PLAN "continues to lack a robust deep-water ASW capability" (CRS RL33153, 2025 [1]).
- [Assessment] CSIS's 24-iteration invasion wargame found the opening strike "destroys most of Taiwan's navy and air force in the first hours", and urged Taiwan to fund coastal defense cruise missiles, missile boats and mines rather than large surface combatants (CSIS, 2023 [5]).

### C2. Aircraft carriers and air wings
- [Fact] Liaoning (2012) and Shandong (2019) are ski-jump carriers. Fujian (about 80,000 t, electromagnetic catapults) was commissioned on 5 Nov 2025, leaving the PLAN with three operational carriers (CRS RL33153, 2025 [1]; Naval News, Jan 2026 [6]).
- [Assessment] Ski-jump launch limits J-15 takeoff weight (about 62,000 lb versus about 100,000 lb from a US catapult carrier), trading weapons against fuel; Liaoning's wing was about 24 J-15s (CSIS ChinaPower, upd. 2020 [7]).
- [Fact] Chinese state media reported J-15T, J-35 and KJ-600 catapult launches and arrested landings on Fujian, adding fixed-wing airborne early warning to PLAN carrier aviation (Global Times, Nov 2025 [8], search only).
- [Assessment] Experts cited by CRS judged Fujian "likely several years away from regular deployments" (CRS RL33153, 2025 [1]).
- [Fact] In June 2025 Japan reported the first simultaneous operation of two PLAN carriers in the Pacific (Japan Times, 2025 [9], search only). Japan's Joint Staff counted about 700 takeoffs and landings from Liaoning (25 May-19 Jun) and about 350 from Shandong (9-19 Jun); a Fuyu-class (Type 901) supply ship sailed with Liaoning (Japan JSO, 1 Jul 2025 [10]).
- [Assessment] The 2025 DoD report says China seeks six more carriers by 2035 (nine in total); a Type 004 carrier is under construction at Dalian (Erickson, WOTR, 2026 [2]; Naval News, Jan 2026 [6]).
- [Fact] No carrier took part in "Justice Mission 2025", the largest drill around Taiwan since 2022 (TSM, 2026 [11]).
- [Assessment] US carriers anchor presence but are exposed: CSIS iterations typically cost the US two carriers, usually the forward-deployed pair "within the first turn or two"; CSIS warned that crisis deployments "make tempting targets" and that lost carriers could not be replaced quickly (CSIS, 2023 [5]).

### C3. Destroyers, frigates, corvettes and fast attack craft
- [Fact] PLAN fleet air defense rests on Type 055 cruisers (12,000-13,000 t; the 10th launched May 2024; a six-hull second batch, four launched in 2025) and Type 052D destroyers (about 7,500 t; total output "approaching 40 hulls") (CRS RL33153, 2025 [1]; Naval News, Jan 2026 [6]).
- [Fact] Thirty Type 054A frigates entered service in 2008-2019; two Type 054B frigates were commissioned in 2025; the 72nd and final Type 056 corvette was commissioned in Feb 2021 (CRS RL33153, 2025 [1]; Naval News, Jan 2026 [6]).
- [Contested] Early Type 056 corvettes were reportedly transferred to the China Coast Guard from 2021; neither service announced the transfer and reported totals vary (about 20-22) (Naval News, 2021 [12], search only).
- [Fact] Taiwan's four Kee Lung-class destroyers are ex-US Kidd-class ships (US service 1981-1998, in Taiwan's navy since 2005) armed with US Standard and Harpoon missiles; its Cheng Kung and Kang Ding frigates mostly carry Taiwanese missiles (Taipei Times, 2018 [13]).
- [Assessment] CSIS (citing IISS 2022) counted 38 major Taiwanese naval vessels (4 submarines, 26 surface combatants, 8 amphibious ships) and called them "outdated ships" with "only rudimentary self-defenses" (CSIS, 2023 [5]).
- [Fact] Taiwan commissioned the Tuo Chiang-class stealth catamaran corvette Dan Chiang in July 2026; all 12 of the class are expected to be operational by end-2026, carrying Hsiung Feng III anti-ship missiles (Defense Post, Jul 2026 [14]).
- [Fact] Taiwan's 12 coast guard Anping-class 600-t catamarans can be fitted with up to 16 HF-2/HF-3 missiles within "several hours" at a military port and then pass to navy command; one fired an HF-3 in Sep 2026 (Baird Maritime, 2026 [15]).
- [Fact] Taiwan bought 100 truck-mounted Harpoon Coastal Defense launchers (four missiles each), 400 missiles and 25 radar vehicles; deliveries began in 2026 and are due to finish by end-2028 (Taipei Times, Aug 2026 [16]).
- [Assessment] In CSIS's games, mobile ground-launched anti-ship missiles survived the opening strike and were "highly effective"; when Taiwan fought alone, shore batteries sank 17 amphibious ships and 17 escorts in 10 days "before being destroyed or running out of missiles" (CSIS, 2023 [5]).

### C4. Amphibious lift, sealift and landing barges
- [Fact] Dedicated lift: four Type 075 LHDs (about 36,000 t), eight Type 071 LPDs, and the drone-capable Type 076 Sichuan (electromagnetic catapult), launched Dec 2024 and in trials since Nov 2025; it reportedly recovered its first unmanned combat aircraft by early Nov 2026, but no commissioning had been confirmed (CRS RL33153, 2025 [1]; Naval News, Jan 2026 [6]; USNI News, Nov 2026 [17], search only).
- [Assessment] Imagery-based reporting indicates six more Type 071-type hulls are under construction (Asia Times, Nov 2026 [18]).
- [Assessment] A Dec 2024 CMSI estimate, as reported secondhand, held that the dedicated fleet could land about one division (about 20,000-25,000 troops) in an initial lift (Asia Times, 2026 [18]).
- [Contested] Civilian ro-ro ferries: Henley argues militia-crewed civilian shipping "could prove good enough" for a large assault (CMSI CMR 21, 2022 [19]); Dahm judges the civilian reserve "probably unable to provide the amphibious landing capabilities" for an invasion through at least 2030 (CMSI CMR 35, 2024 [20]); Shugart estimates ferries and vehicle carriers could move about 300,000 troops in 10 days once a lodgment is stable (as reported in Asia Times, 2026 [18]).
- [Assessment] "Shuiqiao" barges come in sets of three self-propelled units that link into an 820 m relocatable pier; the first set was trialed near Zhanjiang in Mar 2025 and a second was being built; they are "very likely PLA Navy auxiliaries" transmitting no AIS, and could create beach throughput problems (CMSI Note 14, 2025 [21]).
- [Assessment] USCC: large amphibious ships and mobile piers have enhanced China's capacity to blockade or invade "with little advance warning" (USCC, 2025 [22]).
- [Assessment] In CSIS's base case China lost about 138 major ships per iteration, including 86 amphibious ships (about 90% of its total); the US spent its entire LRASM inventory (about 450) in the first week; an invasion only advanced where China captured ports and airfields to raise throughput (CSIS, 2023 [5]).
- [Fact] Justice Mission 2025 was the first time a Type 075 deployed in a joint exercise around Taiwan; no landing was conducted (TSM, 2026 [11]).

### C5. Submarines and anti-submarine warfare
- [Assessment] DoD (2024) counted 6 SSBNs, 6 SSNs and 48 diesel/AIP submarines, projected to 65 boats by 2025 and 80 by 2035 (CRS RL33153, 2025 [1]).
- [Assessment] Naval News estimates about seven new nuclear guided-missile submarines had been produced by late 2025 as output shifts toward nuclear designs (Naval News, Jan 2026 [23], search excerpt).
- [Fact] US officials said the first Zhou-class nuclear attack submarine sank pierside near Wuhan in mid-2024 (AP, 2024 [24], search only); the 2025 DoD report reportedly links the loss to procurement corruption (Erickson, WOTR, 2026 [2]).
- [Fact] Taiwan has four submarines (CSIS, 2023 [5]), of which the two Dutch-built Hai Lung (Chien Lung)-class boats are its combat submarines; by mid-2026 Hai Lung had completed upgrades and sea trials, major work on Hai Hu was done, and 28 Mk 48 Mod 6 torpedoes were due over two years (Taipei Times, Jun 2026 [25]).
- [Fact] Hai Kun, Taiwan's first indigenous submarine, missed its Nov 2025 delivery deadline and accrues daily fines; submerged tests began on 29 Jan 2026 and its 15th sea trial began on 1 Jun 2026; builder CSBC says delivery is "on track" for the second half of 2026 (SCMP/The Star, Feb 2026 [26]; Taipei Times, Jun 2026 [25]; Focus Taiwan, Jun 2026 [27]).
- [Contested] Program politics: opposition legislators froze NT$1.8bn for follow-on boats (part of a NT$284bn plan for seven more) until sea trials end (SCMP/The Star, 2026 [26]); 2023 claims by the program head that a losing bidder leaked information to China led to a prosecutors' probe and libel suits, but I found no outcome (Defense Post, 2023 [28], search only).
- [Assessment] CSIS: Taiwan's submarines are more survivable than its surface ships but vulnerable if surprised in port; a continuous at-sea presence is "currently infeasible due to lack of numbers" (CSIS, 2023 [5]).
- [Assessment] In CSIS's games US submarines entered the strait in every iteration; each sank about two large amphibious ships per 3.5-day turn at roughly 20% attrition, cycling back to Guam or Yokosuka to reload (a "conveyor belt"), but "numbers were inadequate" (CSIS, 2023 [5]).

### C6. China Coast Guard, maritime militia and gray-zone pressure
- [Assessment] DoD: the CCG has more than 150 regional and oceangoing patrol vessels over 1,000 t, more than 50 of 500-999 t and about 300 coastal patrol craft, "the largest maritime law enforcement fleet in the world" (CRS RL33153, 2025 [1]).
- [Assessment] Japan's 2025 defense white paper counts 161 CCG ships of 1,000 t or more (end-2024), including two 10,000-t class, and judges that in a blockade China may deploy its coast guard "at the forefront" (The Diplomat, Jul 2025 [29]).
- [Fact] China's 2021 Coast Guard Law authorizes CCG use of weapons against foreign organizations or individuals infringing what China claims as its sovereignty (RFA, 2021 [30], search only).
- [Fact] After a fatal incident near Kinmen, China announced regular CCG "law enforcement" patrols there; on 19 Feb 2024 CCG officers boarded a Taiwanese tourist boat for about 30 minutes (Maritime Executive, 2024 [31], search only).
- [Fact] Exercise pattern: Joint Sword-2024B brought 153 aircraft, 14 warships and 12 government (largely CCG) ships in 25 hours (Al Jazeera, 2024 [32], search only); Strait Thunder-2025A featured CCG "law enforcement patrols" (Taipei Times, Apr 2025 [33], search only); Justice Mission 2025 brought 89 aircraft and 28 vessels (15 PLAN, 13 CCG) on day one, "integrated blockade and control operations", and five of eight declared zones overlapping Taiwan's territorial waters (TSM, 2026 [11]).
- [Fact] In June 2026 a CCG "special maritime traffic law-enforcement operation" east of Taiwan claimed to have "inspected 198 passing vessels", surveyed, and patrolled near undersea cables; Taiwan said China has no jurisdiction there (Reuters via Baird Maritime, Jun 2026 [34]).
- [Assessment] RUSI: the CCG is positioned to lead a "quarantine" framed as law enforcement rather than naval warfare; withdrawal of commercial insurance is a key constraint on shipping (RUSI, 2026 [35]).
- [Fact] AMTI's satellite counts found a record daily average of 241 militia vessels at Spratly features in 2025; most cannot be reliably tracked by AIS (AMTI, 2026 [36]).
- [Contested] In Dec 2025-Jan 2026 roughly 1,000-2,000 Chinese fishing boats held formations up to about 200 miles long in the East China Sea for 30+ hours; some analysts saw militia blockade rehearsal, others an ordinary fishing ground (Maritime Executive, Jan 2026 [37]).
- [Assessment] ASPI: dredger and fishing fleets "sometimes numbering in the hundreds" near Matsu, plus CCG boardings near Kinmen, overwhelm Taiwan's coast guard and normalize Chinese presence, with deniability for "accidents" (ASPI, May 2026 [38]).

### C7. Blockade, convoy, logistics and mine warfare
- [Assessment] The 2025 DoD report says a Joint Blockade Campaign "would posture air and naval forces to conduct weeks or months of blockade operations" (Erickson, 2025 [3]).
- [Assessment] CSIS "Lights Out?" (26 iterations of 20-week games): blockades "are not bloodless"; most merchant ships will not run a blockade, so cargo is transshipped through Japan to escorted convoys; in the worst case the US and Taiwan lost 33 warships, including two carriers, and 153 merchant ships, and electricity could fall to about 20% of pre-blockade levels; energy is "the weakest element in Taiwan's resilience" (CSIS, 2025 [39]; Defense News, 2025 [40]).
- [Assessment] CRS notes the PLAN would struggle far from the mainland because it "lacks the structure necessary"; its carrier groups rely on a small number of large replenishment ships such as the Type 901 (CRS RL33153, 2025 [1]; Japan JSO, 2025 [10]).
- [Fact] China unveiled the AJX002 extra-large uncrewed underwater vehicle in Sep 2025; official commentary called it an uncrewed mine-laying system featuring "covert emplacement for blockade" (Naval News, Sep 2025 [41]).
- [Assessment] Taiwan has four Min Jiang-class minelayers (10 more planned); holding one port could take 3,000 or more mines; mining also closes ports to one's own trade and might be cast by Beijing as provocation; rocky, fast-current local waters make clearance slow (ASPI, May 2025 [42]).

## F. Subsurface, electronic, space, cyber, information and intelligence

### F1. Undersea operations and maritime domain awareness
- [Assessment] The 2025 DoD report says PRC skywave over-the-horizon radars "can probably detect ships and aircraft" between the first and second island chains (Erickson, 2025 [3]; Erickson, WOTR, 2026 [2]).
- [Assessment] Of two extra-large UUVs paraded in Sep 2025, analysts read the HSU100 as surveillance-oriented and the AJX002 as a mine-layer; the uncrewed underwater fleet adds persistent, deniable undersea presence (Naval News, Sep 2025 [41]).
- [Fact] Much gray-zone traffic is dark or deceptive on AIS: most militia trawlers cannot be tracked by it (AMTI, 2026 [36]), Shuiqiao barges transmit none (CMSI Note 14, 2025 [21]), and the freighter suspected in the Jan 2025 Keelung cable damage may have used two AIS identities (Maritime Executive, Jan 2025 [43], search only).
- [Fact] Commercial satellite imagery now underpins much public maritime monitoring, from militia counts (AMTI [36]) to barge trials (CMSI [21]) and the salvage cranes seen after the Zhou-class sinking (AP, 2024 [24], search only).

### F2. Undersea communication cables
- [Fact] In Feb 2023 both Taiwan-Matsu cables were cut within six days, the first attributed to a Chinese fishing boat and the second to a Chinese cargo ship; Matsu fell back on microwave links and communications were disrupted for weeks until repairs (Foreign Policy, 2025 [44]; Taipei Times/AFP, 2025 [45]).
- [Fact] Chunghwa Telecom recorded 12 Taiwan-Matsu cable severances in 2023, costing NT$96.4 million to repair (Global Taiwan Brief, 2025 [46]).
- [Fact] On 3 Jan 2025 the China-linked freighter Shunxin-39 was suspected of damaging a trans-Pacific cable near Keelung and left after brief detention; on 25 Feb 2025 the Hong Tai 58 severed the Taiwan-Penghu No. 3 cable, and its Chinese captain was sentenced to three years in Jun 2025 after a court found he intentionally anchored in a no-anchor zone (Foreign Policy, 2025 [44]; Global Taiwan Brief, 2025 [46]; Taipei Times, Jun 2025 [47], search only).
- [Fact] Other Jan-Feb 2025 Matsu cable faults were attributed to natural deterioration (Global Taiwan Brief, 2025 [46]).
- [Assessment] Intent is "almost impossible to prove", which gives deniability to repeated "accidents" (Foreign Policy, 2025 [44]).
- [Fact] Matsu's microwave backup carries 12.6 Gbps against a 9.5 Gbps daily peak, with nine satellite sites as a further backup that prioritizes government, banks and hospitals (Foreign Policy, 2025 [44]).
- [Fact] Resilience measures: ten domestic cables designated critical infrastructure (2024); an automatic anchor-warning system on some cables; coast guard boarding of suspect ships within 24 nm; stricter port inspections; and a watchlist of 52 high-risk flag-of-convenience ships (Global Taiwan Brief, 2025 [46]; Taipei Times, Feb 2025 [48], search only).
- [Assessment] Gaps remain: few repair ships, weather delays and an overstretched coast guard (Global Taiwan Brief, 2025 [46]). USCC lists "undersea cable sabotage" among Beijing's tools to weaken Taiwan's will to resist (USCC, 2025 [22]), and the June 2026 CCG operation included patrols near cables (Reuters via Baird, 2026 [34]).

### F3. Electronic warfare and navigation interference
- [Assessment] USCC (2024): PLA EW could "present a significant challenge to US forces by disrupting the data links and communications" they rely on (Defense Post, Nov 2024 [49]).
- [Fact] Taiwan reported J-16D electronic-warfare jets, which carry jamming pods, in its ADIZ in Jan 2022 (Defense Post/AFP, 2022 [50]).
- [Fact] In Apr 2024 the PLA abolished its Strategic Support Force and created separate Aerospace, Cyberspace and Information Support forces (Defense News, 2024 [51], search only).
- [Assessment] The 2025 DoD report judges PLA cyber and non-kinetic operations likely constrained by limited combat experience and the 2024 restructuring (Erickson, WOTR, 2026 [2]).
- [Assessment] ODNI: China fields ground-based counterspace EW systems, lasers and anti-satellite missiles, and BeiDou is a "world-class" navigation service (ODNI ATA, 2025 [52]); the US Space Force lists Chinese jammers aimed at satellite communications, radar and GPS (Space Threat Fact Sheet, 2025 [53]).
- [Speculative] An industry op-ed argues GNSS interference in the crowded strait would be hard to isolate and that China's layered navigation and timing could give it a tempo advantage (Breaking Defense, Jan 2026 [54]). I found no public measurement study of GNSS interference specific to the Taiwan Strait.

### F4. Space-based ISR and communications
- [Assessment] US Space Force: China had more than 1,060 satellites in orbit (Dec 2024), including "510+ ISR-capable satellites" with optical, multispectral, radar and radio-frequency sensors that help it detect US carrier and expeditionary forces (Space Threat Fact Sheet v7, Feb 2025 [53]).
- [Assessment] ODNI: China has "global coverage in some" ISR constellations and began a proliferated LEO broadband constellation in 2024 (ODNI ATA, 2025 [52]).
- [Fact] Taiwan has seven meteorological satellites, one optical imaging satellite and no LEO communications satellites; its space agency says about 150 are needed for "basic communication resilience", and the first of six Beyond-5G LEO satellites is due in 2027 (Taipei Times/AFP, Sep 2025 [45]).
- [Fact] Starlink talks broke down in early 2022 because Taiwan requires at least 50% local ownership; Chunghwa Telecom instead uses Eutelsat OneWeb (since 2023) and SES medium-orbit service, with about 700 receivers planned, and officials judged OneWeb's capacity "too small" (ASPI, Feb 2025 [55]).
- [Assessment] Satellites are an emergency backup to Taiwan's 15 undersea cables, reserved for government, military, hospitals and finance (ASPI, 2025 [55]).

### F5. Cyber operations (strategic level only)
- [Fact] Joint advisory AA24-038A (CISA, NSA, FBI and Five Eyes partners, 7 Feb 2024) says PRC state actors seek to "pre-position themselves on IT networks" in US communications, energy, transport and water systems for disruption in a crisis, in some cases "for at least five years", including on Guam (CISA, 2024 [56]).
- [Assessment] ODNI describes the Volt Typhoon campaign as prepositioning "access on critical infrastructure for attacks during crisis or conflict" (ODNI ATA, 2025 [52]); the 2025 DoD report says it "burrowed into US critical infrastructure" (Erickson, 2025 [3]).
- [Fact] Taiwan's National Security Bureau counted 2.63 million intrusion attempts per day against critical infrastructure in 2025, 6% more than in 2024 (Focus Taiwan, Jan 2026 [57]).
- [Contested] NSB-sourced figures differ by scope: a parliamentary report cited 2.8 million intrusions per day against government networks (+17%) (The Record, Oct 2025 [58]). Both count attempts, not successful compromises.
- [Assessment] The NSB says attempts peaked in May 2025 (the first anniversary of President Lai's inauguration), rise around ceremonies, major statements and leaders' trips abroad, and showed "a certain extent of correlation" with PLA joint combat readiness patrols; energy-sector attacks rose about tenfold, and hospitals were also among the most targeted (Security Affairs, Jan 2026 [59]).

### F6. Information and cognitive warfare
- [Fact] The NSB counted 45,590 inauthentic accounts in 2025 (28,216 in 2024) and 2.314 million pieces of disinformation (2.159 million in 2024) (Taipei Times, Jan 2026 [60]).
- [Assessment] The NSB says the campaign aims to foster skepticism toward the US, Taiwan's military and President Lai and to weaken the "will to resist"; it uses AI-generated content, and hijacked accounts spread false claims during the April 2025 drills, such as a blockade of natural gas shipments (Taipei Times, Jan 2026 [60]; The Record, 2025 [58]).
- [Assessment] Microsoft reported suspected AI-generated audio of a candidate posted on Taiwan's 2024 election day, the first nation-state use of AI content in a foreign election it had seen; the content gained little reach (Microsoft MTAC, Apr 2024 [61], search only).
- [Assessment] USCC: PRC propaganda seeks to "sow doubt about the U.S. commitment to Taiwan" (USCC, 2025 [22]).
- [Contested] During Justice Mission 2025 the PLA released footage it said showed a drone near Taipei; Taiwan's MND disputed it (TSM, 2026 [11]).

### F7. Intelligence and warning
- [Assessment] USCC (2025): US and Taiwanese officials warn a blockade could start "within a matter of hours" with "minimal conversion time"; large unannounced drills make preparations harder to detect (USCC, 2025 [22]; Taipei Times, Nov 2025 [62]).
- [Assessment] Adm. Paparo (INDOPACOM): PLA maneuvers "are not exercises... They are rehearsals" (USNI News, Feb 2025 [63], search only). Japan's white paper likewise says some unification operations may be being rehearsed (The Diplomat, 2025 [29]).
- [Fact] Justice Mission 2025 was announced at about 0730 on the morning it began; Taiwan's MND said it had noticed irregular activity several days earlier (TSM, 2026 [11]).
- [Assessment] ASPI's wargame indicators: red-line rhetoric, cyber and influence surges, drills straying beyond usual zones, field hospitals, fuel and ammunition gathering at embarkation points, and reserves mobilizing at night; an invasion drill "could quickly evolve into full mobilisation" (ASPI, Jul 2025 [64]).
- [Assessment] Economic indicators alone are weak and prone to false positives; they must be paired with military and propaganda signals (CSIS, 2022 [65]).
- [Assessment] Capability is not intent: CIA Director Burns said Xi ordered the PLA to be ready by 2027 but "that does not mean that he's decided" to invade (AP, 2023 [66], search only); ODNI sees "steady but uneven progress" (ODNI ATA, 2025 [52]).
- [Assessment] The evidence base is itself uncertain: hull counts rest on open sources and official figures lag by a year or more (CRS RL33153, 2025 [1]; Erickson, WOTR, 2026 [2]).

## Design implications

The mechanics below are design proposals for the fictional Celestine Commonwealth (mainland), Republic of Tessera (island) and Hesperian Federation (distant ally). Each label marks the strength of the evidence behind the mechanic, not the mechanic itself.

- [Assessment] **Carriers supply only part of the air effort.** Give Celestine carriers a sortie cap per turn: ski-jump carriers pay a payload penalty, the catapult carrier adds an early-warning bonus, and carrier endurance only refills when a supply ship is in the group. Land-based air and missiles should dominate raids near the strait [5][7][10].
- [Assessment] **Forward-deployed allied carriers are a deterrence gamble.** Moving a Hesperian carrier group forward in a crisis raises deterrence but exposes it to an opening strike. Lost carriers cannot be replaced within the campaign [5].
- [Assessment] **Amphibious groups are slow, scarce and must be found and engaged in time.** Each sunk amphibious ship permanently removes lift. Celestine needs a captured port or a deployed barge-pier to unlock civilian follow-on lift, and the barges should be fragile and weather-limited [5][18][21].
- [Contested] **Civilian ferries are a surge resource with an asterisk.** Let Celestine requisition ferries (raising lift but generating warning indicators). Their usefulness should depend on port access and a training level, reflecting the expert split over how much they can do [18][19][20].
- [Assessment] **Coastal missile batteries survive by moving and die by running dry.** Dispersed batteries should survive the opening strike, then lose effectiveness through limited missiles, the need for radar or targeting data, and gradual attrition, as in CSIS's "17 amphibious ships plus 17 escorts in 10 days" result [5][16].
- [Assessment] **The player's surface navy is brittle in harbor.** Ships caught in port suffer heavy opening-strike losses. Dispersing to sea costs readiness. Coast guard cutters can convert to missile boats in "several hours", but converted cutters stop policing gray-zone incursions [5][15].
- [Assessment] **Submarine contacts create friction more than kills.** Uncertain contacts should force escorts, reroutes or delays on convoys and amphibious groups. Hesperian submarines should follow a strike, return-to-reload, return cycle with attrition each pass, while Tessera fields only two or three boats [5][25][26].
- [Assessment] **Coast guard "inspections" are an escalation dilemma.** Run quarantine as a law-enforcement track: Tessera can shadow, hail and expel, escort, ignore or fire, and each choice moves an escalation meter and an international-support meter. Foreign merchants may comply or divert, and insurers can withdraw, cutting trade [29][34][35].
- [Contested] **Militia swarms saturate rather than fight.** Use many low-value, AIS-dark tokens that absorb coast guard patrol capacity and mask other movements, with an intelligence check needed to tell fishing fleets from militia [36][37][38].
- [Assessment] **Blockade is an energy clock with escalation built in.** Track fuel stocks that drive electricity and morale, a transshipment port in an allied state, merchant willingness and escort demand. Blockade turns should carry casualty and escalation risks, not just economic effects [39][40].
- [Assessment] **Mining is a double-edged defensive card.** Tessera can mine port approaches to slow landings, at the cost of closing its own ports and handing Celestine a pretext. Celestine drone mine-laying is covert and hard to attribute, and clearance takes many turns [41][42].
- [Fact] **Cable cuts degrade communications and intelligence for several turns.** Model them as gray-zone events: island links fall back to low-capacity microwave or satellite backup reserved for government use, repair depends on repair ships and weather, and attribution stays uncertain, so there is no automatic casus belli. Watchlists, boarding and patrols lower the odds [44][46][47][48].
- [Assessment] **Cyber access is banked in peacetime and spent in crisis.** Treat Celestine pre-positioning as a hidden stockpile that can disrupt power, ports or communications when activated. Daily attack counts are background noise; defensive investment reduces effects and attribution arrives late [52][56][57].
- [Assessment] **Electronic warfare lowers data quality rather than destroying units.** Jamming should reduce data-link quality and targeting accuracy, with EW aircraft escorting raids and investment in alternative navigation and timing as the counter [49][50][54].
- [Assessment] **Space surveillance is persistent but imperfect.** Celestine satellites should give a high detection chance for large ships in the open and lower odds for small, dispersed or AIS-dark units. Counterspace can blind the other side, while Tessera relies on allied or commercial imagery and capacity-limited satellite links [45][52][53][55].
- [Assessment] **Cognitive warfare targets will and alliance trust.** Disinformation surges around drills and political dates should erode a "will to resist" and an alliance-confidence meter. Rapid public rebuttal and platform action reduce the effect, and most AI-generated content gets little reach [22][60][61].
- [Assessment] **Warning is probabilistic and exercises look like the real thing.** Intelligence should report indicators (red-line rhetoric, drills outside usual zones, field hospitals and fuel at embarkation points, night reserve call-ups, ferry requisition) with confidence levels. Snap drills can be announced the same morning, and false alarms cost readiness and money [11][22][64][65].
- [Assessment] **Assessments lag, carry ranges and are sometimes wrong, and capability is not intent.** Intelligence summaries should arrive a turn late with confidence bands. Celestine's shipbuilding depth should let it replace hulls far faster than the Hesperian Federation [1][2][52][66].
